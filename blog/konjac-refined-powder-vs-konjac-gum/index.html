The short answer

Refined konjac powder and konjac gum are related konjac ingredients, but their names alone do not tell you how one grade will perform against another. Compare the declared composition and its test method, processing history, hydration behaviour, measured viscosity and performance in your food. A higher composition value does not, by itself, establish faster hydration, stronger gel formation or a lower dose. [1] [2]

For procurement, use the name to begin the discussion. Use a grade specification, a batch certificate of analysis (COA) and a formulation trial to make the decision. This article explains the technical comparison; our ingredient enquiry guide explains what to send to a supplier.

1. Start with the name—and the specification behind it

Konjac glucomannan (KGM) is the polysaccharide associated with the thickening and texture functions of konjac ingredients. In commercial discussions, konjac flour, refined konjac powder, purified konjac flour and konjac gum can describe materials with different compositions and processing histories. They are not a universal ladder with a single purity or viscosity assigned to each name. [1] [2]

China has separate standards titled Konjac refined powder (GB/T 18104-2000) and Konjac flour (NY/T 494-2010). Their official records establish the document identities; a purchase specification still needs the relevant clauses and test methods. An English sales description is not enough to establish equivalence between standards or regulatory categories. [3] [4]

2. How processing can change the material

Milling, separation, extraction, washing and drying can change the amount of non-glucomannan material and the characteristics of the resulting powder. Processing can also affect polymer structure. A process name therefore needs to be read alongside composition and functional measurements. [1] [2]

EU specifications illustrate why precise terminology matters: E425(i) konjac gum is defined through aqueous extraction from konjac flour, while E425(ii) konjac glucomannan is defined through washing konjac flour with water-containing ethanol. These are distinct entries; an informal description such as “alcohol-purified gum” does not establish which entry a product meets. [1]

Particle behaviour and final solution behaviour also need separate attention. In a study of seven purified konjac flours, particle size and surface morphology were associated with hydration behaviour, while final viscosity depended on additional structural factors. That study does not establish a universal mesh size or a performance ranking for every commercial grade. [2]

3. Read composition numbers without mixing the test bases

“KGM content”, “carbohydrate” and “total dietary fibre” are not interchangeable labels for a single measurement. Before comparing percentages, identify the analyte, method and reporting basis.

EU specification entry Composition criterion stated in the specification What the number does not establish
E425(i): konjac gum At least 75% carbohydrate A universal “75% KGM purity” grade or a final-food performance guarantee
E425(ii): konjac glucomannan At least 95% total dietary fibre on a dry-weight basis A measured KGM assay of exactly the same meaning, or compliance with every other specification and use condition

Source: Commission Regulation (EU) No 231/2012, E425 entries; the distinction is also documented in EFSA’s re-evaluation. These are selected composition criteria, not a complete compliance checklist. [1] [5]

For example, a supplier’s “90% KGM” statement should be accompanied by its method and basis. That number alone neither proves nor disproves compliance with a total-dietary-fibre criterion. Request the relevant test results rather than converting one label into another.

Additive identity and purity specifications are also separate from permission to use an ingredient in a particular food category. EU rules do not establish US requirements. Confirm the intended food, use and destination market before ordering.

4. Compare viscosity only when the methods match

A quote of “20,000 mPa·s” is incomplete without its method. Record the following for both samples before treating their viscosity values as comparable:

  • Sample and concentration: grade, batch, concentration, and whether the concentration is based on powder as supplied or dry matter.
  • Water and formulation: test medium, pH and any salts or other ingredients.
  • Preparation: addition order, dispersion method and mixing conditions.
  • Time: hydration time, rest time and the point at which the reading is taken.
  • Temperature: temperature during hydration and at measurement.
  • Instrument: model, geometry or spindle, rotational speed and the reported reading procedure.

A spindle number without the instrument model is not a complete method. Nor should a particular temperature or rotation speed be presented as the universal method for every konjac grade. Use the method required by the applicable specification, or an agreed method suitable for your comparison.

Keep three questions separate: How quickly does it disperse and hydrate? What viscosity does the hydrated system reach? Does the finished food have the required texture? The answers can lead to different grade choices. [2]

These are evaluation questions, not fixed dosage recommendations. Do not use a universal replacement ratio between refined powder and gum. The trial must establish the dose and processing conditions needed to meet the same target.

Assess cost in use, not only price per kilogram. Start with unit price multiplied by the working dose per batch, then account for any measured differences in process time, yield and quality. A more expensive powder is economical only if the validated result justifies its cost.

6. A practical supplier-comparison checklist

Ask each supplier for the same information so the offers can be compared on a common basis:

  1. Identity and traceability: product name, grade, batch number, specification version and applicable standard.
  2. Composition: analyte, limit, batch result, analytical method and reporting basis.
  3. Functional method: complete viscosity method plus any agreed hydration or gel test.
  4. Physical quality: particle-size distribution or sieve specification, colour and odour limits, and the evaluation method.
  5. Safety and market documents: applicable microbiological, contaminant and residue results for the grade and intended market, including processing-solvent information where relevant.
  6. Supply conditions: packaging, documented shelf life and storage instructions, sample evaluation and batch-consistency arrangements.

Request results for the proposed material. A generic certificate, an unqualified “food grade” label or a claim that the “highest purity is always best” does not replace this comparison.

Once the material is identified, use the ingredient enquiry guide to prepare a project brief. For a deeper explanation of hydration, viscosity and gel testing, read our KGM formulation guide.

7. Frequently asked questions

Is konjac gum always higher in KGM than refined konjac powder?

Do not decide from the names alone. Compare the specified analyte and test method for the actual grades. Regulatory definitions and commercial labels do not always use the same measurements. [1] [3] [4]

Does finer powder always hydrate better?

Particle characteristics can affect hydration, but practical dispersion also depends on mixing and addition conditions. Compare the hydration curve and lump formation in your process rather than selecting solely by mesh count. [2]

Can I replace one grade with another at the same dose?

Treat substitution as a formulation change. Compare samples under the same conditions, then establish the dose that achieves the same finished-food target. No fixed exchange ratio is supplied by the product names.

Does a high-purity grade automatically qualify for health claims?

No. Ingredient identity, permitted use, product composition and the conditions for any claim are separate questions. A composition percentage is not permission to make a health claim for the finished product.
